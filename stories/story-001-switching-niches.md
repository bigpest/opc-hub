# Switching a Settled Account to a New Niche

I am fifty. Twenty-odd years in backend engineering, most of it inside one industry, plus the certificates that come with that many years. In 2026 I found myself without a job and with an account that already had a topic, and I decided to sell resume reviews to people who are where I was fifteen years ago.

This is the first week, written while it is still unresolved.

## Starting point

Two things were already in place before I started, and both of them were liabilities I did not count.

**The account.** Thirty-three followers, forty-six posts, all on a single theme: passive income, AI monetisation, paid knowledge products. It had a settled identity. When I opened the account every post was in the same visual language and the same voice, and the platform had a working theory about who should see it.

**The credential.** Twenty years of reading, screening and being screened. I have sat on both sides of a hiring table. That is the only real asset I brought, and it is the one thing most people selling resume advice do not have.

I picked resume and interview work for two reasons. It passes the tests for a one-person service — the delivery is repeatable, no licence is required, the customer is findable in public — and the audience is enormous because the anxiety is enormous. Everyone between twenty-two and forty-five has a resume they do not believe in.

I priced it as an entry offer at 200, with two tiers above it at 398 and 598, deliberately not tiering by audience. Tiering a student at 99 and a senior engineer at 398 would have anchored the average order value at the bottom and glued a "cheap services" label onto an account that is supposed to be a knowledge account.

## What I tried, in order

**1. I wrote eight posts before publishing anything.** Four for graduates, two for engineers with one to three years, two for senior engineers. The plan was a thirteen-day publishing schedule.

**2. I built covers as HTML, not as AI-generated images.** Chinese large-type covers come out of image models with broken glyphs. Six pages at 1080×1440, rendered by a headless browser, gave me something legible at thumbnail size.

**3. I published the first two posts a day apart.** First post: 25 views, one comment, zero saves. Second: 14 views, nothing.

**4. On the third day I published three posts, two of them thirteen minutes apart.** This was a mistake, and I knew it while doing it. The reasoning that let me do it was "different audiences, so no cannibalisation" — which is wrong. At this account size there is no separate audience; there is one small pool.

**5. I changed the shape of the content on day three, not the wording.** The first three posts explained why a resume fails. The last two handed over a structure the reader could copy: a project split into three lines, five substitutes for an internship. I also moved the search term into the title, so the post can be found by someone typing the problem rather than only by someone scrolling past it.

**6. I published the paid offer before I had a way to be paid.** The offer post describes the 200 service in detail — three deliverables, three exclusions — and there is no store listing behind it yet. Someone who wants to buy has to find a private route, which is exactly the route that gets a new account restricted.

## What it cost

- **Time.** About six hours to write eight posts, four hours on covers, and roughly an hour per post doing the publishing by hand because the platform has no scheduling I trust.
- **Money.** Nothing. The cost was not financial.
- **The part that hurt.** Watching post one get 25 views. Twenty-five. I have given talks to rooms bigger than that. The instinct after that number is to rewrite the post. The number has nothing to do with the writing: at thirty-three followers, 25 views is a normal result and rewriting it is a way to spend a week feeling productive without changing anything.

The other thing that hurt was realising the account had a tag and I had been building on top of it as if it did not. Forty-six posts of passive-income content is not neutral ground. It is a statement about what the account is for.

## What actually happened

Five posts over five days. Views are the numbers the platform showed me; saves are the column that matters at this size.

| Post | Shape | Views | Saves |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| "80 applications, no interview, died on the first screen" | Explanation | 25 | 0 |
| "Three years of experience, written like month one" | Explanation | 14 | 0 |
| "Nine years, the word *responsible* 17 times" | Explanation | 9 | 0 |
| "How to write project experience — one project in three lines" | Copyable | 12 | 1 |
| "No internship — five substitutes you already have" | Copyable | 6 | 1 |

Two findings, both of which I would not have believed before running it.

**Saves track the shape, not the reach.** The explanation posts reached two to four times as many people and produced nothing that anyone kept. The copyable posts reached fewer people — one of them only six — and each produced a save. At 6 views and 1 save, the save rate is absurd in a good way. That is not a vanity metric; a save is the platform's only signal that says "this is worth keeping", and at a cold start it is the one thing I can influence.

**The account tag is the ceiling, not the writing.** Nine to twenty-five views is what a settled account gives a new topic. Nothing in the copy changes that. It will move when enough posts on the new topic accumulate, or it will not move at all.

As of this writing there is no paid client. The offer post went live this morning. I am not going to describe an outcome I have not had.

## What I would tell someone starting today

1. **Count your account's history before you plan anything.** If it has forty-six posts on another topic, your first five posts on the new one are a cold start with an extra handicap. Plan for it instead of being surprised by it.
2. **Publish one post a day, maximum.** Not because of a platform rule, but because your audience at this size is one pool and two posts a day split it.
3. **Write the shape that gets saved, even if it gets fewer views.** A kept post keeps being distributed after day one. A read post stops.
4. **Put the payment path in place before you publish the price.** Otherwise the most motivated readers — the ones who would have paid — have nowhere to go, and the ones who arrive are the ones who want to negotiate in a private message.
5. **Do not rewrite copy because reach is low.** Reach is account history. Copy fixes saves, not reach.

---

This account may still fail. The niche may not transfer, the tag may not move, and one save on two posts is not a result. But the shape comparison is real and it happened in five days, and that is worth writing down before the ending is known.
