Most one-person companies do not fail because the idea is bad. They fail because the delivery model quietly assumes a team: someone to answer support, someone to sell, someone to fill in the gaps. The direction you pick decides whether one person can deliver at all.

This is a filter to apply before you build anything.

## Who this is for

- You have no capital, no team and no unusual credentials.
- You are choosing a first direction, or replacing one that stalled.
- You want revenue this year, not after a funding round.

## Five filters

A direction is viable for one person if it passes all five:

1. **The delivery is reusable.** You build, write or record something once and sell it many times. Selling hours fails the test on the first busy month.
2. **Startup cost is survivable.** You can reach the first paying customer for roughly the price of a laptop.
3. **Customers are findable in public.** Search, marketplaces, communities, ranking pages. If the only way in is a warm introduction you do not have, the direction is closed to you.
4. **No licence is required.** Regulated fields — medicine, finance, licensed education, bookkeeping, staffing, travel agency work — are not open to an individual, and working around the requirement is a way to lose the business later.
5. **Eighty percent is worth paying for.** If the customer needs the last twenty percent to get value, and that last twenty percent needs a team, you will ship something nobody wants.

## Five categories that pass

| Category | Concrete forms | Startup cost | Revenue shape | Real bottleneck |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Productised skill** | Résumé and interview coaching, niche social media or SEO management, video and podcast editing, localisation, custom planning services | Low | Project → monthly retainer | Getting the first three case studies |
| **Content and knowledge** | Niche industry newsletter, single-skill course, templates and asset packs, paid community, podcast | Very low | Subscription or one-off | Consistency — stop publishing and it dies |
| **Software and small tools** | Niche browser extension, mini app, mobile utility, no-code SaaS, an AI workflow packaged as a product | Low, plus your time | Subscription or one-time licence | Distribution, not development |
| **Digital goods and light commerce** | Stock assets, 3D models, commissioned crafts, regional speciality products sold through content | Low to moderate | Product margin | Commoditisation; differentiation lives in content and sourcing |
| **Brokering and information** | Industry introductions, matching services, authentication and appraisal | Very low | Commission or fee | Trust takes years; know where the legal line is |

## What to avoid

- **Dropshipping at scale and cross-border catalogue selling.** One platform policy change resets you to zero.
- **Mass-produced AI content.** Supply exploded, price went to near zero, and platforms now penalise it.
- **Franchises, agencies and reseller programmes.** You are buying a customer-acquisition script, not a business.
- **Anything that requires a licence you plan to borrow.** The liability stays with you.

## How to choose

1. **List your three unfair advantages.** An industry you worked in, a skill other people find tedious, a group of people who already trust you. "Three years inside one industry" counts. It does not need to be impressive.
2. **Narrow until it is searchable.** Not "marketing", but "point-of-sale content for independent dental clinics". Not "English teaching", but "quote negotiation for export sales staff".
3. **Sell the service before you build the product.** Take three clients by hand. Confirm they pay and that the process can be written down. Only then turn client four onward into a template, tool or course. People who start with a SaaS product usually die of nobody knowing it exists.

## Time and money

- First revenue: 2–8 weeks if you sell a service to people you can already reach.
- First repeatable revenue: 3–6 months, mostly spent on distribution rather than building.
- Cash needed before revenue: under a few thousand in most of the categories above. If a direction needs more, that is information.

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing a skill with a market.** Being good at editing does not tell you who pays for it.
- **Choosing a direction that requires daily output you cannot sustain.** Content-led models fail from fatigue more often than from competition.
- **Pricing as if you were an employee.** A solo operator has no bench, so a low hourly rate compounds into a worse job than employment.
- **Waiting for the product to be finished.** The service version is the product until customers tell you otherwise.

## One thing worth remembering

In 2026 the barrier to entry in all of these categories is falling, while the barrier to being noticed is rising. Whether you can keep producing something that lets strangers find you matters more to the outcome than how clever the idea is.

---

This is a decision framework, not a case study. Treat the filters as a way to rule directions out quickly; the evidence has to come from your own first three customers.
