# First Clients Without an Audience

A productised skill needs someone to buy it, and the standard advice — build an audience first, then sell — takes months you do not have. This is what I did instead, and what it cost.

## Who this is for

- You sell a service one person can deliver: resume review, interview coaching, editing, localisation, a niche audit.
- You have no list, no followers worth counting, no referrals.
- You want a paying client this month, not after a content marathon.

## The mistake I made first

I had a small account already — 33 followers, 46 posts, all on an unrelated topic. My plan was to post valuable content for a few weeks, wait for followers, then introduce the service.

Two things went wrong.

First, the account carried a topic tag from those 46 posts. The platform had a settled idea of who should see my posts, and it was not people looking for career help. New posts on the new topic got roughly the reach a brand-new account gets: 6 to 25 views. Nothing was broken; that is simply what a cold start looks like.

Second, I had written the kind of content I liked reading — long explanations of why a resume fails. Those posts got the most views and zero saves. The posts that handed over something copyable got fewer views and, for the first time, saves.

In a cold start, saves are the only signal you control. A view depends on how many people the platform decides to show it to. A save depends on whether the thing you wrote can be used later.

## What works instead

Treat your posts as a filter, not as marketing. Every post should make exactly one kind of reader think "that is my problem" and make everyone else scroll past. A post that appeals to everyone converts nobody and teaches the algorithm nothing.

### Step 1: pick five specific failures, not five topics

Not "how to write a resume". Instead: "the first screen is read in eight seconds and yours says nothing", "three years of work written like the first month", "the word *responsible* appears 17 times and means nothing".

Each post names a failure precisely enough that a reader either has it or does not.

### Step 2: make every post carry something copyable

The failure post explains. The copyable post converts. A post that only explains gets read and forgotten; a post that includes a template, a three-line structure, a checklist, a before-and-after gets saved — and saved posts keep being distributed after the first day.

Concretely, end with a thing the reader can paste into their own document, not with a question. A question buys comments; a template buys saves.

### Step 3: state the offer, with its boundaries

Put the price, what is included, and what is explicitly excluded in the post itself. "One diagnosis and one rewrite; no ghostwriting, no invented experience, no promise of interviews" does more work than any amount of reassurance, because it answers the objection the reader is already forming.

Do not hide the price until a private conversation. Hiding it costs you the readers who would have paid and keeps the ones who were never going to.

### Step 4: use comments as the qualifying step, not direct messages

Ask for one specific sentence in the comments — "which section of your resume do you feel least confident about" — rather than "DM me". Comments are public, they cost the reader nothing, and they tell you who is actually in the market. Direct-message solicitations with contact details are the fastest way to get a new account restricted.

### Step 5: deliver the first three by hand

Take the first three clients manually, at whatever price they will pay, and write down every step you performed. The written-down version becomes the product. Starting with a packaged product usually fails, not because the product is bad but because nobody knows it exists.

## Evidence: five posts, one account, five days

Same account, same visual style, two content shapes. Views are cumulative as of day five; saves are the only column I care about.

| Post shape | Views | Saves |
|---|---:|---:|
| Explanation — first screen | 25 | 0 |
| Explanation — three years written as one | 14 | 0 |
| Explanation — *responsible* ×17 | 9 | 0 |
| Copyable — project split into three lines | 12 | 1 |
| Copyable — five substitutes for an internship | 6 | 1 |

The explanation posts reached more people and produced no saves. The copyable posts reached fewer people, one of them only six, and each produced a save. Until the account's topic tag changes, saves are the lever that exists.

## Time and money

- Five posts: roughly six hours of writing, zero cash.
- First paying client: two to eight weeks when you can already reach the audience, longer from a cold account.
- What you actually spend is attention, not money.

## Pitfalls

- **Posting several pieces of the same kind on one day.** Two posts thirteen minutes apart split the same small audience and each ends up weaker than either would alone.
- **Rewriting the copy because reach is low.** Reach at cold start is a function of account history, not of the writing. Change the shape of the content, not the adjectives.
- **Confusing a niche switch with a content problem.** An account with a settled tag needs several posts before the tag moves. Three posts is not evidence.
- **Publishing a price with no way to buy.** If the only route to payment is a private message, you have built a funnel that ends in a violation. Open a store listing or a payment page before you publish the price.
- **Waiting for the audience.** The audience arrives after the first clients, not before.

## One thing worth remembering

In a cold start the question is not "how do I get more views". It is "what can I publish that a stranger would keep". Views are the platform's decision. Whether your post is worth keeping is yours.
